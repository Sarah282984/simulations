The Deep Door

Edmond and Elise need to design a submarine to reach the ocean floor, 2,000 m down. It needs to be strong enough to withstand the pressure, and big enough to fit them inside. It can't be so heavy that the motor can't lift it, or so light that the motor can't sink it, either. Try out different materials, shapes, and sizes, weigh it down with ballast if you need to, and then do test runs to see how deep you can get!

How real is this?

The sea, the pressure and the forces in this game use real physics. Whether your submarine breaks is worked out with simpler versions of the equations real engineers use, so shapes and materials behave the way real ones do. The exact depths are estimates, though. Real submarines are much more complicated. Engineers also have to think about tiny cracks, joints, windows and hatches, and they build in extra strength to stay safe.

For grown-ups: how the sim works out what breaks

The sea is set so pressure rises by exactly 1 atm for every 10 m, starting at 1 atm at the surface. Real seawater is very close to this (about 1 atm per 10.06 m), so the rounding is about half a percent.

  • Sphere. Imagine cutting it in half. The water pushes on the half with a force equal to pressure × the flat circle πR² (the half-sphere's shadow, because the tilted parts of the curved surface mostly push sideways and cancel). The ring of wall where the halves meet carries that force, so the average wall stress is force ÷ ring area (about PR/2t for a thin wall). In a thick wall the inner face carries more than the average, so the sim uses the thick-wall (Lamé) formula for the stress at the inner face. For thin walls the two agree.
  • Capsule (a tube with rounded ends, the shape of most real submarines). Cut it the long way. Water pushes on a flat rectangle 2R × length, and two strips of wall hold it, so the stress is about PR/t, twice the sphere's. The sim uses the thick-wall (Lamé) formula here too.
  • Crumpling (buckling). Thin shells can fold inward before the material itself is crushed. Sphere: classical shell formula, multiplied by 0.25 because real spheres buckle at a fraction of the textbook value. Capsule: the Windenburg–Trilling formula for tubes under outside pressure, times 0.8. These are approximate.
  • Flat walls (prisms and the box) bend. Side walls use the standard clamped-plate formula (Roark), and the flat ends are treated as clamped plates too. Bending squeezes one face of the wall and stretches the other, which is why glass and granite crack: they are very strong squeezed and weak stretched. The flat-wall numbers are the roughest part of the model.
  • Materials (approximate): steel 450 MPa, brass 250 MPa, glass 700 MPa squeezed / 40 MPa stretched, granite 180 / 10 MPa, oak 40 / 20 MPa. Chocolate is the joke material, with rough guesses: 1.3 tonnes per m³ (so it sinks), 8 MPa squeezed, 2 MPa stretched. It can't survive the deep sea at any size. As a check, the model puts a real 17-inch deep-sea glass float sphere (14 mm wall, rated for 6,700 m) at about 8,350 m before it fails, a plausible margin above its rating.
  • Supplies. Steel is limited to 2,000 kg. A steel design that needs more than that can't be built, so it can't go in the tank or the sea. There is plenty of every other material.
  • Ballast is lead, added 100 kg at a time (up to 3,000 kg). It adds weight without changing the outside shape, so it doesn't add lift. It takes up a little room inside (100 kg of lead is about 9 litres).
  • Weight and lift. Lift is the weight of the water the whole outside shape pushes aside. Weight is the hull plus 150 kg for Elise, Edmond and their gear, plus any ballast, plus the air inside. The motor can push about 300 kg up or down, so the difference has to stay within ±300 kg to dive and return.
